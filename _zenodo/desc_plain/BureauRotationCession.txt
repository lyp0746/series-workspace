Abstract.Chinese officialdom displays a recurring shape: a low-ranking attendant, censor or deputy acquires real power in a crisis; after its holder deposes an emperor, monopolises authority or secedes, the office is proscribed and hollowed out, and power migrates to a new low-ranking post. Existing scholarship documents the facts but leaves three questions open: why proscription never ends the cycle; why the inner-court and outposted paths produce different failure modes; and why designers repeatedly separate military from edict authority. Using the completeness–composability–transfer (C–A–T) triangle as an analytical instrument (a framework, not a theorem; precondition ψ&gt;0), the paper operationalises the three requirements, states seven falsifiable propositions, and codes 121 offices across 24 periods from the Western Zhou to 2026. Findings: the three paths correspond to three two-vertex combinations; adding A costs C, while not adding A weakens cross-dynasty transfer; proscription has five forms, all targeting the name rather than the configuration; the number of parallel lines does not predict stability—the common factor is a “fusion line” holding all three vertices; temporal mismatch τ explains why delegation is necessary, not why it fails; daily rotation is second-order while fusion lines produce first-order collapse; and there are four evolution modes. Three negative results are reported, including the retraction of the author’s earlier claim that coordinates sum to a constant.
